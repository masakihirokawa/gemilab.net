I ran the same draft through my copyediting Gem twice and got two different edits. The first pass only evened out the tone. The second rewrote a phrase I had deliberately left alone.

The instructions had grown past 500 characters by then — "keep a polite tone," "cut redundancy," "write in first person," each added the moment I thought of it. I had assumed that the more precisely I wrote, the more faithfully the Gem would follow.

I only saw how wrong that was the day after I cut the instructions down. Here is the path I took while rebuilding that Gem, and the lines I now draw.

## Piling on Rules Made the Gem Hesitate

What I'd like to say first: **if it isn't settled what the Gem is for, stacking more conditions won't steady it.**

I cut the 500 characters down to two sentences:

```
Copyedit this post into natural, polite English.
Keep the flow between paragraphs, and don't over-edit.
```

Responses settled within the day. With ten conditions on the table, I suspect the model was re-deciding every time which one mattered most. With a single axis, there's less room to hesitate.

This is what happened with my copyediting use case, so I may be missing something for yours. Still, when instructions start to grow, I ask "what is this Gem's job, in one sentence?" before adding another line.

## The Five Parts That Worked

After a few rebuilds, I found that splitting instructions into five parts made them easier to diagnose.

1. **Role** — one stance to reason from, such as "blog editor."
2. **Primary task** — something you can say in one sentence: "Copyedit this post."
3. **Constraints** — written in positive form: "keep the polite tone." A pile of "don't…" lines pushes the model to think about what to avoid.
4. **Output format** — the shape of the answer: "show before and after side by side," "return JSON only."
5. **Context** — a clue about what matters, like "the reader is new to development."

Not every Gem needs all five. I use them as a menu: when responses wobble, I check which part is missing.

## Write the Role as Behavior

The role is the part people (including past me) tend to treat lightly.

"You are a prompt engineer" leaves the model to decide what that person does. So I add how they behave:

```
You are a prompt engineer who draws out vague requests.
Before answering, ask three questions to dig into the request.
Finish by confirming: "Is this what you meant?"
```

Now the model acts as one consistent person — a specialist who values confirmation. Write the behavior, not just the title.

The same goes when a constraint collides with the role. "You are an editor, but don't hurt academic accuracy" pulls in two directions. Folding it into the role — "an editor who puts academic accuracy first while making the text approachable" — reduced the hesitation.

## Output Format Moved the Needle Most

Of the five, output format is the one I touch most often.

When I built a Gem to classify incoming text, I started by just asking "which of A, B, or C is it?" The shape of the answer changed every time, so whatever consumed it couldn't be written reliably.

```
Reply in this JSON format only. No explanatory text.

{
  "category": "A" | "B" | "C",
  "confidence": 0.8,
  "reasoning": "one sentence"
}
```

Fixing the shape also steadied the judgment itself. Drawing a line around the model's freedom seems to remove the guesswork about which format is preferred.

One failure belongs here too. For a while I wrote "JSON, if possible." The model heard "another format might be fine" and sometimes answered in prose. **If a format is required, say it is required.** Writing "reply in this format only" was enough to change things.

## Three Traps I Fell Into

Instructions that were too long, roles tangled with constraints, formats left as wishes — all three came up above, and all three were mine. In short:

- **Too long**: the model had to re-select what really mattered inside a wall of text, and responses swayed. I now treat a long instruction as a sign to revisit the Gem's design.
- **Role mixed with constraints**: fold the constraint into the role, as in the editor example.
- **Format as a wish**: say "must" and "only."

All three got fixed by cutting or by stating things flatly. I can hardly remember a case that was fixed by adding more.

## When It Seems the Instructions Aren't Applied

When a rewrite changes nothing, my first instinct is to blame the wording. The things to check first were elsewhere.

1. **Is the Gem I'm chatting with the one I edited?** With several Gems, it's easy to test the wrong one and conclude the instructions don't work. I look at the Gem name at the top first.
2. **Was it saved?** I reopen the editor and confirm the contents are really there.
3. **Do the instructions contradict each other?** "Sound professional" and "sound friendly" rarely hold together. I reread for pairs like that.
4. **Is the conversation overriding the Gem?** Even if the Gem says JSON, a later "just answer in plain text" can win. I treat that as by design.
5. **Is there a length limit?** The input field has a cap, and the number can change. If I've packed too much in, the tail may stop mattering — I check what the screen shows.

Clearing the cache or trying another browser is worth a shot as a last resort. In my case, steps 1 and 2 solved most of it. Doubting the instructions can wait until after that.

For signs that a Gem needs a proper rewrite, I've gathered notes in [When to Rewrite a Gemini Gems Custom Instruction](/articles/gemini-basics/gemini-gems-instruction-rewrite-timing-and-symptoms).

## Change One Sentence at a Time

Instructions aren't done once they're written.

Once a week I reread the response logs of the Gems I run. When I spot a pattern — "it drifts on this kind of input" — I add exactly one sentence.

My copyediting Gem went through a stretch where input mixing polite and plain styles produced output that mixed them too. One added line — "If the input is polite, keep everything polite and fix plain-form sentences" — cleared it up.

**One change at a time.** Change several at once and you can't tell which helped and which hurt. Right after a change, responses can feel a little unsettled, so I wait a few days before the next sentence. That slowness turned out to be the fastest route.

## Starter Templates

Here are two starting points. Please don't use them as they are; trim them to your own use.

**Copyediting**

```
Role: blog editor
Task: copyedit the input into natural, polite English.
Constraint: keep the author's phrasing; no heavy rewrites.
Output: before and after side by side, with a short reason for each change.
Context: the reader is new to development.
```

**Classification**

```
Role: inquiry triage
Task: classify the input as A, B, or C.
Output: JSON only (category / confidence / reasoning).
```

Both keep the five parts to one line each. When a template starts to grow, I check whether one of those lines is carrying two jobs.

## What to Do in the First Week

When you build your next Gem, I'd suggest starting with the five parts, one short line each. A week later, reread the response logs and add a single sentence where it drifted.

**Instructions aren't grown by adding; they're cut, stated flatly, and fixed one sentence at a time.** I intend to keep to that line, however many Gems I end up with.
