"Gemini gives different people different answers to the same question." If that observation rings true, you're not imagining it. I spent most of last year handing the same prompts to Claude and Gemini and watching Claude nail them while Gemini wandered off-course. For a long time I called Gemini "the weaker model." The honest read, after a lot of debugging, is that I was writing prompts Claude could interpret but Gemini wouldn't.

Google's internal guidance has converged on a structured-prompt framework called RSFC — Role, Situation, Format, Conditions. Using it consistently made the quality gap between Claude and Gemini almost disappear for me. This post is a deep, practical walkthrough of each element plus six templates you can adapt tomorrow.

## Why Gemini rewards structure

Gemini is less willing than Claude to fill in context you didn't specify. Think of Claude as "reads between the lines" and Gemini as "responds to what's actually written." Neither is objectively better, but they're different design philosophies — Gemini is tuned to be faithful to the literal prompt.

The practical consequence: "just get it" prompts work less well. "Here's the frame I want you to respond inside of" works much better. RSFC is the structure that locks that frame in.

## The four elements

### Role

Who should Gemini be when it answers? This isn't a costume — it's the most powerful lever you have, because it sets vocabulary, depth, and default tradeoffs.

**Weak:** "You're an engineer."  
**Strong:** "You're a senior iOS engineer with 10+ years of experience, writing for both solo developers and fellow engineers. You've shipped Swift 6 concurrency in production and understand the messy reality of migrating existing projects."

The strong version works because the specifics — "10 years", "Swift 6", "migrating existing projects" — narrow Gemini's vocabulary toward the kind of language that person would use. Vague roles produce vague responses.

### Situation

What's happening, and what constraints apply? Eighty percent of the information mass in a good prompt lives here.

**Weak:** "Fix a bug in my app."  
**Strong:** "An existing iOS 18 budget-tracking app migrated to Swift 6. It's crashing when Core Data saves on a background context. Firebase Crashlytics shows about ten reports per week, clustered just after users return from the expense-entry screen to the dashboard."

Include who's affected, when it happens, how often, and any partial reproduction steps. Without this specificity, Gemini has no choice but to answer in generalities.

### Format

What shape do you want the response in? Skip this and Gemini defaults to freestyle Markdown — sometimes helpful, often hard to process downstream.

**Weak:** "Use bullets."  
**Strong:**
```
Respond in this format:

## Hypotheses (up to 3, with confidence %)
## Reproduction steps (include minimal code)
## Investigation order (cheapest failure first)
## Proposed fix (diff format, comments in English)
```

An explicit structure makes downstream use — quoting in another document, pasting into Slack, feeding into Claude Code — much smoother.

### Conditions

What must be true, and what must not happen? Include negative constraints (what to avoid) here.

**Example:**
```
- Use Swift 6 syntax only
- Do not use Swift 5.x syntax
- Prefer async/await; no DispatchQueue
- English comments only (no Japanese)
- Don't propose new external libraries; use existing deps
- Keep the tone neutral and technical
```

Think of Conditions as the contract. It's also your rubric when you evaluate the response.

## Three templates for solo developers

### Template 1: Code review

```
# Role
You are a senior reviewer with 5+ years of production TypeScript 5.4, Next.js 16, and Tailwind CSS. You weight accessibility and performance equally.

# Situation
The React component below will be used for the sidebar of /[locale]/dashboard in a personal project. On mobile (<768px) it must become a drawer. The current code has two `any` uses and incomplete WAI-ARIA coverage.

```tsx
[paste code]
```

# Format
- Group findings by severity (Critical / Major / Minor)
- For each, give a rationale and a diff
- End with an ordered fix priority list

# Conditions
- Always flag `any`
- Assume shadcn/ui (don't propose alternative UI libraries)
- Be specific about where aria-label is required
- Total length under 1,500 characters
```

### Template 2: Product decision

```
# Role
You are a hybrid PM / engineer who has designed features for multiple solo-dev SaaS products. You're fluent in the tradeoffs between free and paid tier boundaries.

# Situation
Dolice Labs (four AI blogs) sells premium articles via a Pro monthly plan ($5/mo) and a Premium lifetime plan ($15). Non-member article purchasers are asking to share premium content on social media.

# Format
- User-experience impact (pros / cons)
- Implementation difficulty and effort (Small/Medium/Large)
- Qualitative revenue impact
- Final recommendation (GO / NO GO / more validation)

# Conditions
- Don't gut the revenue model
- Preserve perceived value for existing members
- Assume Next.js 16 + Cloudflare Workers
- Briefly address legal/privacy at the end
```

### Template 3: Exhibition statement

```
# Role
You are a copywriter who works across contemporary art and advertising, including English translations of artist statements for overseas shows.

# Situation
Masaki Hirokawa (developer/artist) is planning a fall 2026 solo show in Tokyo. Theme: "Self-portrait in the AI era." Media mix: painting, video, interactive.

# Format
- Statement in Japanese (~400 chars)
- Statement in English (~250 words)
- Key visual direction (color, composition, typography)
- Three social captions

# Conditions
- Avoid gratuitous art-world jargon
- Weave in the developer background naturally
- English must read as native, not translated
- Max five hashtags per language
```

## Two templates for business

### Template 4: Competitive landscape

```
# Role
You're a researcher who runs 30+ competitive analyses per year in consumer SaaS.

# Situation
We operate a personal-finance app in Japan. ~100k MAU, 2% paid conversion. We want a qualitative comparison with Zaim, Moneyforward ME, and OsidOri.

# Format
| Dimension | Zaim | MF ME | OsidOri | Us |
| Pricing | | | | |
| UI differentiation | | | | |
| Unique features | | | | |
| Threat level | | | | |

Close with three concrete moves to make in the next 90 days.

# Conditions
- Timestamp your sources
- Label speculation as "estimated"
- Separate facts from opinion
```

### Template 5: Executive one-pager

```
# Role
You're a technically-fluent PM who routinely explains complex topics to non-technical executives.

# Situation
Internal pitch for adopting RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation). The audience has moderate IT literacy — "AI" means ChatGPT to most of them. Annual budget ceiling: ~$20k.

# Format
- ~2 pages of prose
- 3-line summary at the top
- Describe any diagrams in words (actual graphics to be added later)
- Three FAQ items at the end

# Conditions
- Define technical terms on first use
- Show business value with concrete numbers
- Cover the risk of NOT adopting RAG too
- Acknowledge vendor-lock-in concerns
```

## One template for research

### Template 6: Fact-checked summary of English-language news

```
# Role
You're an editor with 10 years of experience summarizing English-language tech coverage for a Japanese audience. You prioritize recency and source verification.

# Situation
Preparing a Japanese-audience summary of Claude Design (announced April 2026). Pull from the official announcement, international tech press, and SNS reactions.

# Format
- Summary (<= 5 sentences)
- Facts (with source URLs)
- Speculation / opinion (unsourced items)
- Local market implications

# Conditions
- Clearly separate fact from speculation
- Label unverifiable items "unverified"
- Give Japanese gloss on English proper nouns at first mention
- Keep your own opinion out of every section except the last
```

## When to break RSFC

RSFC isn't universal. Three places I actively deviate:

**1. Creative brainstorming.** Short-form poetry, concept generation, campaign copy — too strict a Format strangles divergent thinking. Keep Role and Conditions; write `Format: respond freely` and let Gemini range.

**2. Exploratory problem scoping.** When the spec isn't settled and you want Gemini to push back, heavy Format constraints push it to answer anyway. Keep Role and Situation; write `Format: start by asking about anything you're unsure of.`

**3. Long-running conversations.** Ten turns in, re-declaring full RSFC each turn makes Gemini forget prior agreements. Treat the turn as an incremental instruction on top of the established context instead.

## Pick the right model

Even the best prompt loses to the wrong model choice.

| Use case | Recommended model | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Code review and refactoring | Gemini Pro | Long context + reasoning |
| Strategy and planning | Gemini Pro | Multi-angle analysis |
| Everyday Q&A and light writing | Gemini Flash | Cost and latency |
| Image generation | Nano Banana Pro | Latest image quality |
| Large batch work | Gemini Flash | Token economics |
| Complex agentic flows | Gemini Pro + Antigravity | Agent optimization |

## One habit to start tomorrow

Don't try to adopt all four RSFC elements at once. Start by forcing yourself to write a richer Situation. Five things in every prompt: who, when, where, what's broken, what you've already tried. That single change moved my Gemini answers up perceptibly.

Build to the others week by week — Format in week two, Role in week three. The moment that converted me was watching my first specific Role rewrite an answer in the vocabulary of the profession I described. Once you feel that, you'll never go back to "you're an engineer."
