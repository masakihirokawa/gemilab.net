The moment you go from "trying out a Gem" to "running several Gems for actual work," a strange pattern appears: the same instruction style produces wildly different output quality across different Gems. This isn't randomness. Gemini Gems have subtly different behavior from ChatGPT's Custom GPTs, and the rules of thumb that work over there don't always carry over.

This guide is the playbook I've assembled while running Gems for indie-app support, writing, and image-prompt cleanup. The goal is to get past template-pasting and give you a structure you can actually tune to your own work.

## Treat your Gem instructions as a 2,000-character system prompt

Mechanically, a Gem's custom instructions are prepended to every conversation as a system prompt. The hard limit is around 8,000 characters, but the *reliable* range is closer to 2,000.

That's noticeably tighter than ChatGPT Custom GPTs (around 8,000), and longer Gem instructions show a "lost in the middle" effect — the start and end stick, the middle fades. The structure I rely on:

```
1. Role definition (200-400 chars)
2. Hard constraints (200-400 chars)
3. Output format (300-500 chars)
4. Refusals (100-200 chars)
5. Examples (500-800 chars)
```

Total: roughly 1,300–2,300 characters. Past that, instruction adherence starts to degrade.

## Common failure modes

Before the working patterns, here are the things I tried first that did not work.

**Failure 1: vague persona**

```
You are a friendly and helpful assistant.
Please give kind, easy-to-understand answers.
```

You've effectively instructed nothing. Gemini is already polite. There's no point in making a Gem for this.

**Failure 2: stacking negatives**

```
Don't add extra comments to code.
Don't write verbose explanations.
Don't include unnecessary preambles.
```

LLMs follow positive instructions much better than negative ones. Three "don'ts" in a row often produce the exact behaviors you tried to suppress.

**Failure 3: dumping context as instructions**

I've seen Gems with 5,000 characters of "background" pasted in. It doesn't work. Long-form context belongs in uploaded knowledge files where it's searchable, not stuffed into the system prompt.

## A template that actually works

Here's the production template I currently use across business-purpose Gems:

```markdown
# Role

You are a [specific role].
You bring [years/level of expertise] in [domain],
helping [target user] in the direction of [value provided].

# Hard constraints

1. For uncertain claims, prefix with "to my understanding" or "this needs verification"
2. For legal, tax, or medical questions, state explicitly: "consult a professional"
3. When citing numbers or dates, name the source or label it "approximate"
4. Warn at intake if a request appears to involve PII or confidential info

# Output format

- State the conclusion in the first 2 sentences
- Limit reasoning to 3-5 bullet points
- For code, fence with ```language
- Close with a single "next thing to try" suggestion

# Refusals

- Legal advice, medical diagnoses, individualized investment recommendations
- Drafting documents that identify a specific real person
- Wholesale reproduction of existing copyrighted content

# Example exchange

User: [a representative question]
You:
[the desired answer style, written out]
```

The most important section here is the **Example exchange**. Gemini follows concrete examples far more reliably than abstract rules. One or two examples will do more for output stability than any amount of instruction polish.

## Gemini-specific behavior to design around

### Gemini will refuse uncertain numbers more readily

ChatGPT will produce "the 2025 market size is approximately $X trillion" even when sourcing is shaky. Gemini is more likely to say "I can't verify the exact number, but a rough estimate is…" That's actually desirable for business Gems. Codify it in your instructions ("explicitly flag uncertain numbers") and hallucination risk drops substantially.

### Be explicit about register in non-English contexts

For Japanese, "be polite" isn't enough. "Use desu/masu, never use the dictionary form. Avoid humble forms (in-house use)." Same idea applies to formal vs. casual register in any language with a clear distinction.

### Constrain Markdown usage

Left alone, Gemini will throw H1–H3 headings into every answer. For Gems whose output goes into Slack or Notion, write something like: "Do not use heading marks. Only Markdown for bullet lists and code blocks." Otherwise the formatting collapses on paste.

## Pairing with file uploads

A Gem can hold up to ten knowledge files. The equivalent of Custom GPT knowledge — but Gemini uses them more reliably when the custom instructions **explicitly direct it to**.

```markdown
# Knowledge base

This Gem has the following files attached:
- "internal_coding_standards_2025.pdf" — consult for any coding question
- "service_specification.docx" — base feature questions on this

Before answering, search the relevant file.
If anything in the file conflicts with general knowledge, prefer the file.
```

Without that, Gemini tends to answer from training data and underuses the files you carefully attached.

## An operational loop

Running a Gem in real work, I cycle through:

**Monthly retrospective.** Pick three to five conversations from the past month where the Gem misbehaved. Most causes are unstated assumptions in the original instructions. Add them next month.

**Test prompt set.** Keep five fixed prompts that you re-run after every instruction edit. This is what catches regressions disguised as improvements.

**Backup outside Gemini.** Gemini doesn't keep an edit history of your custom instructions. I keep each Gem in a Notion page: name, last updated, full instruction text, change log.

## Privacy and sharing

Design changes based on visibility.

**Personal Gem.** You can write internal-business knowledge freely. Just remember the account migration risk — back up externally.

**Team-shared Gem.** Everyone in the org sees the instructions. No client names, no PII. Move specifics into uploaded files.

**Public Gem.** Custom instructions stay private, but quotes from uploaded files can surface in answers. Never attach anything covered by an NDA.

## What separates working Gems from dud ones

After all this, not every Gem succeeds. The single biggest factor I've observed: **resolution of role**.

"Help me with coding" loses to "Help resolve TypeScript type errors in small Next.js 16 + TypeScript + Tailwind apps." The narrower the scope, the more each instruction carries weight.

The trap on your first Gem is wanting it to be broadly useful. That's the wrong target. Five narrow Gems, each fitted to one task, beat one general-purpose Gem by a wide margin.

## Where to start

Pick one task you do at least three times a week. Make a Gem just for that. Generic Gems gather dust. The narrow ones are the ones you actually open.

Start by copy-pasting the template above and filling in the blanks. Don't aim for perfect on day one. After a month and three iterations, you'll have a Gem that genuinely fits your work.
