In the spring of 2019, I looked up from a platform at Kichijoji Station and saw a ring of light floating in the sky.

A circular arc hung quietly against the overcast clouds — something I couldn't explain with ordinary logic. From that moment, something shifted in how I approach visual expression. The structure of light, the meaning inscribed in space, the direction a viewer's gaze is drawn. Whether I'm painting or designing a UI, that question has stayed with me ever since.

So when I asked myself "how does Gemini Vision read an image?" it felt genuinely worth exploring. My work has received 17 international art awards. I wanted to know how accurately an AI could interpret it. Here's what I found after testing it from late 2025 through early 2026.

---

## The 4 Artworks I Gave to Gemini Vision

I deliberately chose 4 pieces from my portfolio to cover a range of interpretive scenarios:

- **Work A**: An abstract piece on the theme of light diffraction. Colors extracted from photos taken at a Shinto shrine, digitally reconstructed
- **Work B**: A long-exposure photograph capturing crowd movement. Intended to visualize collective psychology
- **Work C**: A composition heavy on negative space. Features textures that mimic washi (Japanese paper)
- **Work D**: A pattern-based piece that performs strongly in my wallpaper app — part of a series from an app that has surpassed 50 million cumulative downloads since I started building indie apps in 2014

I spread the selection across works with clear intent, multiple interpretive layers, concealed intent, and commercial success — to see how Gemini handles each.

## The Analysis Code (Python)

I used Gemini 2.5 Flash for all four analyses. Flash is fast and cost-effective for batch image work.

```python
import google.generativeai as genai
from pathlib import Path
import json, base64

genai.configure(api_key="YOUR_GEMINI_API_KEY")
model = genai.GenerativeModel("gemini-2.5-flash")

def analyze_artwork(image_path: str, mode: str = "general") -> dict:
    """Analyze an artwork image using Gemini Vision."""
    
    prompts = {
        "general": """
            Analyze this image visually. Respond in JSON with these fields:
            - "dominant_colors": 3-5 dominant colors (hex code + name)
            - "composition_style": Describe the composition in 3 sentences
            - "emotional_tone": The emotional tone (e.g., introspective, tense, calm)
            - "visual_focus": Where does the viewer's eye go first?
            - "cultural_hints": Any cultural or regional elements visible?
            - "keywords": 5-7 English keywords usable as tags
        """,
        "wallpaper_fit": """
            Evaluate this image as a smartphone wallpaper. Respond in JSON:
            - "suitability_score": Score 1-10
            - "target_mood": Ideal user mood or situation for this wallpaper
            - "icon_readability": Impact on icon visibility (good/average/poor)
            - "suggested_category": Suggested wallpaper category
            - "improvement_hints": Any improvement suggestions (if any)
        """
    }
    
    image_bytes = Path(image_path).read_bytes()
    encoded = base64.b64encode(image_bytes).decode()
    ext = Path(image_path).suffix.lower()
    mime_map = {
        ".jpg": "image/jpeg", ".jpeg": "image/jpeg",
        ".png": "image/png", ".webp": "image/webp"
    }
    mime_type = mime_map.get(ext, "image/jpeg")
    
    response = model.generate_content([
        prompts[mode],
        {"mime_type": mime_type, "data": encoded}
    ])
    
    text = response.text
    start, end = text.find("{"), text.rfind("}") + 1
    if start != -1 and end > start:
        return json.loads(text[start:end])
    return {"raw_response": text}

# Example usage
result = analyze_artwork("artwork_a.jpg", "general")
print(json.dumps(result, indent=2))
# Expected output:
# {
#   "dominant_colors": [{"hex": "#F5E6C8", "name": "warm white"}, ...],
#   "emotional_tone": "introspective, quiet",
#   "visual_focus": "slightly above center"
# }
```

Each image took 1.5–3 seconds and cost roughly $0.00002 per image (Flash pricing, May 2026).

## What It Got Right, and What It Missed

Here's the honest breakdown:

**Work A (light diffraction, abstract)**: The analysis was surprisingly accurate. The 5 dominant colors matched my intent closely, and the emotional tone came back as "introspective, quiet" — which fits. But for cultural context, it returned "Japanese Zen aesthetic." My actual theme is "exploring the structure of collective psychology and cognition through the lens of distinctly Japanese forms of prayer." The surface calm was detected, but the underlying tension was not.

**Work B (crowd movement, long exposure)**: This was the most interesting result. Gemini identified "where the viewer's eye goes first" as the light source in the upper left — not the central movement I intended. Looking at the piece again with fresh eyes, there actually is a gravitational pull toward that corner. The AI surfaced a compositional element I hadn't consciously noticed.

**Work C (negative space, washi texture)**: Keywords returned included "minimalism," "Japanese aesthetic," "meditative." Not bad. But the wallpaper suitability score came in at 6/10, with "icon readability: average." The art reading was reasonable, but it was conservative about functional fitness as a wallpaper — more cautious than I would have been.

**Work D (pattern series, commercial hit)**: Wallpaper suitability: 9/10. "target_mood: relaxing for everyday use." "suggested_category: Nature / Texture." This aligned closely with the actual category data from my app. It was a useful confirmation that the AI's commercial instincts are calibrated reasonably well.

## Practical Applications for My Wallpaper App

After this experiment, I integrated Gemini Vision into three parts of my workflow.

### Auto-generating App Store descriptions

Writing individual descriptions for wallpaper images was a time sink. Now I generate them from the Vision analysis:

```python
def generate_app_description(analysis: dict, locale: str = "en") -> str:
    """Generate App Store copy from Vision analysis output."""
    model = genai.GenerativeModel("gemini-2.5-flash")
    
    if locale == "en":
        prompt = f"""
        Write a 50-word App Store description for a wallpaper app.
        Based on this analysis: {json.dumps(analysis)}
        Evoke the scene where someone would use this wallpaper. Include natural SEO keywords.
        Avoid excessive promotional language.
        """
    else:  # ja
        prompt = f"""
        以下の分析データをもとに、壁紙アプリ向けの日本語説明文を 80 字前後で書いてください。
        ユーザーが使うシーンを想起させ、SEO キーワードを自然に含める形で。
        分析データ: {json.dumps(analysis, ensure_ascii=False)}
        """
    
    return model.generate_content(prompt).text.strip()
```

The output tends to describe "a user's morning with this wallpaper" rather than the artwork itself — a user-facing perspective I don't naturally write from.

### Auto-tagging and performance prediction

Using `suggested_category` and `keywords` together, the auto-tagging accuracy sits at around 85–90%, with me reviewing the remaining 10–15%. I also matched `suitability_score` against actual download data: works scoring 7 or above consistently performed better. It's not a perfect predictor, but it works as a first-pass filter.

## Where Gemini Vision Falls Short

Worth being honest about the limits.

**It can't read artistic intent**: Gemini reads form and color well, but the conceptual meaning behind a piece doesn't come through. The theme of "the structure of collective psychology" in Work A never appeared in any analysis output. For interpretations that require cultural context, creator background, or conceptual knowledge, human judgment is still necessary.

**It conflates artistic value with functional value**: Whether something works as a wallpaper and whether it works as art are two different questions. Gemini answers the first question reasonably. It struggles with the second.

**It can't capture the quality of light**: The ring of light above Kichijoji — the kind of visual experience that resists verbal description — comes back as "glare effect" or "lens flare like pattern" when photographed and analyzed. The functional description lands, but the core of the experience doesn't. This is a confirmation, not a complaint. Knowing exactly where the boundary sits makes the tool more useful.

## Did Gemini Vision Actually See My Work?

My artwork centers on a theme I'd describe as: exploring collective psychology, cognitive world structures, and primal consciousness against the backdrop of distinctly Japanese forms of prayer. I work primarily with photography and video, and I've received 17 awards through international competitions including the IAA (International Art Awards), ARC Salon, and London Photo Festival.

I sent several pieces from this body of work to Gemini Vision API with the prompt: "Analyze what this image is trying to communicate."

What came back was a careful articulation of color distribution, compositional balance, light and shadow relationships, and an overall tone described as "contemplative" and "still."

**What it got right**

Color temperature and directional lighting were analyzed with impressive accuracy. Observations like "there's intentional tension between the foreground and background" and "focus softens progressively from center to edges" matched precisely what I was thinking during production. Most strikingly, the interpretation that "space itself is the subject, not any individual figure" came close to the conceptual core I care about most.

**Where it fell short**

The concept of "prayer" as it's understood in Japanese culture remains thin to Gemini. It collapsed into "spiritual atmosphere" — a perfectly adequate English translation that nonetheless loses the particular quality of tension within stillness, the sense of reverence toward the unseen, that characterizes East Asian devotional aesthetics.

This isn't really a critique of Gemini specifically. It's a structural artifact of English-dominant training data. Interestingly, prompting in Japanese produced slightly different — sometimes more nuanced — results.

## Implementing Artwork Analysis with Gemini Vision API

Here's the core implementation I used to send images and retrieve analysis:

```python
import google.generativeai as genai
from pathlib import Path

genai.configure(api_key="YOUR_GEMINI_API_KEY")

model = genai.GenerativeModel("gemini-2.5-pro")

def analyze_artwork(image_path: str, prompt: str = None) -> str:
    """
    Analyze an artwork image using Gemini Vision.
    image_path: Path to the image file (JPEG/PNG supported)
    prompt: Analysis focus (defaults to general art analysis if omitted)
    """
    if prompt is None:
        prompt = """
        Analyze the visual characteristics and themes this artwork communicates.
        Please address the following:
        - Composition and use of space
        - Color palette and light quality
        - Emotional tone and atmosphere
        - Themes or narratives the work suggests
        Respond in English.
        """
    
    image_data = Path(image_path).read_bytes()
    
    # Determine MIME type from extension
    suffix = Path(image_path).suffix.lower()
    mime_map = {".jpg": "image/jpeg", ".jpeg": "image/jpeg", ".png": "image/png"}
    mime_type = mime_map.get(suffix, "image/jpeg")
    
    response = model.generate_content([
        {"mime_type": mime_type, "data": image_data},
        prompt
    ])
    
    return response.text

# Example usage
result = analyze_artwork("my_artwork.jpg")
print(result)

# Sample output:
# This work establishes a strong horizontal axis that divides the frame
# between a distant background and a detailed foreground.
# The light source is positioned upper-right, casting shadows consistently
# toward the lower-left. The palette approaches monochrome — low saturation
# throughout — which lets the absence of figures paradoxically assert presence.
# The mood is still and inward, yet carries an undercurrent of tension.
```

Response times typically fall between 2 and 5 seconds. Per-image cost with Gemini 2.5 Pro is roughly $0.0001–$0.0004 depending on resolution and token count. Artwork tends to be on the higher end since it contains denser visual information than typical wallpaper images.

## Connecting to Wallpaper App Development

Beyond the art analysis experiment, Gemini Vision proved genuinely useful in the context of running my wallpaper apps. With over 50 million cumulative downloads, I process large volumes of images on a daily basis. Decisions about "which images will resonate with users" and "how to categorize them correctly" had previously relied on manual review or rule-based classifiers.

Switching to Gemini Vision, the standout use case was **color tone consistency checking**. Prompts like "Does this wallpaper have a morning-light quality or an evening-light quality?" returned answers that aligned closely with human judgment. Similarly, "Will notification icons be readable over this wallpaper at typical contrast levels?" produced practical, reliable responses.

The limitation appeared with genre-specific Japanese aesthetics. Categories like "healing" (癒し系) or "law-of-attraction" (引き寄せ系) carry cultural connotations that don't map cleanly to English-language training data. Bridging that gap required prompt engineering tuned to these specific nuances. ## The Asymmetry in "Seeing"

The most interesting finding from this experiment wasn't about technical precision.

When AI "sees," it matches visual inputs to existing, verbalized patterns — binding color, texture, and composition to known concepts. When an artist "sees," the aim is often to reach something that resists existing language. When I make work, I'm trying to leave something that cannot be said on the surface.

Gemini is remarkably good at saying that unsaid thing. That is both an impressive capability and a moment where a fundamental asymmetry becomes visible.

In 2019, I saw a ring of light above a train station in Kichijoji. It was the moment I became certain that visual expression was where I needed to work. Since then, I've approached making with the assumption that intuition precedes language — that the image leads, and words follow afterward. Gemini runs this sequence in reverse: starting from language patterns and working toward the visual. Neither direction is wrong, but the difference raises a genuine question about what "seeing" as a verb actually means.

Does Gemini "see" a work, or does it "read" it? The answer might be both, or neither. But the question itself sharpened my thinking about my own practice.

## The Value of Imperfect Interpretation

Despite these observations, I've continued using Gemini Vision API.

The reason is simple: imperfect interpretations generate useful friction.

When Gemini's reading doesn't match my intention, it becomes a productive question — "why did it read it that way?" Following that question often leads back to something I put into the work without fully noticing. In that sense, Gemini's misreadings have become part of my production feedback loop.

My grandfathers on both sides were temple carpenters (宮大工). Growing up watching them, I absorbed a sense that working carefully with one's hands is a form of devotion. I find that same quality in writing code and in making art. Interacting with AI is starting to feel like another variation of it.
