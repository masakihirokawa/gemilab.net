I sorted last week's search data by impressions and looked at the top of the list. The first five rows all had zero clicks.

My first instinct was that the titles were weak. Decent positions, nobody clicking — that usually points at the headline.

Then I actually read the queries.

`rork.com core business features target audience` — I could not picture a person typing that into a search box.

## The five queries sitting at the top

The site in question is one I run alongside my work as an indie developer: a technical site covering app development tooling. Total impressions for the period were 41,700.

That is 5,449 impressions, or 13.1% of the total, with zero clicks across the board.

Three of the first four read wrong. `core business features target audience` is a stack of attribute nouns. `features and ai capabilities` follows the same shape. `onspace vs rork for complex app logic` even carries its own comparison axis inside the phrase.

People do not search that way. A person types `rork max pricing` — short, only the words they need.

These are sub-queries that an AI search layer generated internally. One question comes in, and the system decomposes it into several retrieval queries to assemble an answer. How many depends on the question; Google has not published a fixed number. The behaviour is [documented as query fan-out](https://digiday.com/media/wtf-is-query-fan-out-in-googles-ai-mode/), and Google describes [how impressions are counted on AI-powered search surfaces](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/ai-features) — but the report does not separate them from human searches for you.

So my CTR denominator contains impressions that structurally cannot produce a click.

## Three assumptions worth dropping first

Before touching any code, I want to clear away three things I believed going in. Each of them shows up in plenty of SEO write-ups, and I had absorbed all three without checking. (Checked against Google's own explanations and the Search Console UI, November 2026.)

**"CTR fell, so the title is weak."** CTR is a ratio. If clicks stay flat and non-human impressions get added underneath, CTR drops without the title changing at all. Read the denominator before you rewrite anything. I nearly skipped this step in the opening scene above.

**"Search Console's AI-related filtering will tell me which queries are synthetic."** Search Console has been adding ways to look at appearances on AI features, but that answers *where* you appeared, not *who* produced each query row. As far as I can tell, nothing in the Queries table marks a row as a fan-out sub-query. That gap is exactly why you need your own layer that reads the wording.

**"Position inside an AI answer block means the same as a normal position."** On surfaces like AI Overviews, where several links appear together in one block, Google has explained that the links in that block share the same position. A row with a flattering average position and no clicks may not be "ranking high and ignored" — it may simply be one of several links counted at the block's position.

All three were avoidable by reading the metric's definition first. **Read numbers without their definitions and they bend into the shape of what you were hoping for.** That was the lesson this whole exercise kept handing back to me.

## Never put click count in the classifier

I nearly walked into the obvious trap here.

"Zero clicks plus high impressions" would make a tidy rule. All five rows above satisfy it.

But apply that rule and CTR rises the moment you exclude anything. You are removing exactly the impressions that contribute nothing to the numerator. That is not an improvement; it is arithmetic engineered to flatter itself.

I have built metrics like that before — the kind that always move in the direction you were hoping for. The danger is not the first wrong number. It is that once a metric starts agreeing with you, you stop checking it.

So I decided the classifier would ignore clicks entirely and look **only at the shape of the query string**. If a high-click query ever gets flagged as synthetic, that tells me the rule is wrong, and I want that failure mode to stay visible.

## Scoring queries on word order alone

This runs directly against the `Queries.csv` you export from Search Console. No API access required.

```python
# fanout_score.py - score a query for "AI fan-out likeness" using the string alone
# Deliberately ignores clicks and impressions to avoid circular reasoning
import csv
import re
import sys
from dataclasses import dataclass, field

# Function words that chain attributes together; rare in human queries
JOINERS = (" vs ", " versus ", " for ", " and ", " with ", " without ")

# Words people use when something is broken. Strong negative signal.
INTENT_MARKERS = (
    "error", "fix", "not working", "failed", "how to", "why", "cannot", "can't",
    "エラー", "できない", "直し方", "とは", "使い方",
)

# A trailing year usually means a human wanting current information
YEAR = re.compile(r"\b(19|20)\d{2}\b")

# Nouns that ask a system to enumerate; skewed toward generated sub-queries
ATTRIBUTE_NOUNS = re.compile(
    r"\b(features|capabilities|use cases|target audience|overview|benefits)\b"
)


@dataclass
class Verdict:
    query: str
    score: int
    band: str
    reasons: list = field(default_factory=list)


def fanout_score(query: str) -> Verdict:
    q = query.lower().strip()
    tokens = q.split()
    score, reasons = 0, []

    if len(tokens) >= 6:
        score += 1
        reasons.append(f"tokens>=6 ({len(tokens)})")
    if len(tokens) >= 8:
        score += 1
        reasons.append(f"tokens>=8 ({len(tokens)})")

    joiners = [j.strip() for j in JOINERS if j in f" {q} "]
    if joiners:
        score += 1
        reasons.append("joiner:" + "/".join(joiners))

    hits = [m for m in INTENT_MARKERS if m in q]
    if hits:
        score -= 2
        reasons.append("intent:" + "/".join(hits))

    if YEAR.search(q):
        score -= 1
        reasons.append("year-suffix")

    if ATTRIBUTE_NOUNS.search(q):
        score += 1
        reasons.append("attribute-noun")

    # 3 or more: fan-out. 1 or less: human. 2: undecided.
    band = "fanout" if score >= 3 else ("human" if score <= 1 else "ambiguous")
    return Verdict(query=query, score=score, band=band, reasons=reasons)


def main(path: str) -> None:
    with open(path, newline="", encoding="utf-8-sig") as f:
        for row in csv.DictReader(f):
            v = fanout_score(row["Top queries"])
            print(f"{v.band:9s} score={v.score:2d}  {v.query}")
            print(f"            {v.reasons}")


if __name__ == "__main__":
    main(sys.argv[1] if len(sys.argv) > 1 else "Queries.csv")
```

            ['tokens>=6 (6)', 'attribute-noun']
fanout    score= 3  rork max swiftui features and ai capabilities
            ['tokens>=6 (7)', 'joiner:and', 'attribute-noun']
ambiguous score= 2  onspace vs rork for complex app logic
            ['tokens>=6 (7)', 'joiner:vs/for']
human     score= 1  expo audio continue playing screen locked react native 2025
            ['tokens>=6 (9)', 'tokens>=8 (9)', 'year-suffix']

            ['tokens>=6 (8)', 'joiner:and', 'attribute-noun', 'tokens>=8 (8)']

Two out of five came back with any confidence.

My eyes said four of these were obviously machine-written. The scorer caught fewer than half. That gap was the opposite of what I expected. What I was reading was the *seating* of the vocabulary; what the scorer reads is token count and function words. Those are not the same measurement.

And lowering the threshold to close the gap immediately swallows row four, which is a perfectly legitimate search. This is not a tuning problem.

## Sending only the undecided rows to Gemini

The two `ambiguous` rows go to the model. Not all five — and the reason is reproducibility rather than cost. Whatever a deterministic rule can settle should stay with the rule; only the judgements that genuinely require reading language go to the model.

The output has to be structured. Free-form text breaks whatever aggregates it downstream.

```python
# adjudicate.py - send only the ambiguous rows to the model
from google import genai
from pydantic import BaseModel

client = genai.Client(api_key="YOUR_API_KEY")

SCHEMA_PROMPT = """You are analysing search logs.
Decide how likely it is that a human typed the given query into a search box.

Guidance:
- Human queries are short and contain only the words the person needs
- Stacked attribute nouns (features / capabilities / target audience) and
  phrases with the comparison axis embedded are typical of AI-generated
  sub-queries
- A long query that looks like a pasted error message is human

You are given no click or impression data. Judge from the wording alone."""


class Judgement(BaseModel):
    likely_human: bool
    confidence: float  # 0.0-1.0
    reason: str


def adjudicate(query: str) -> Judgement:
    response = client.models.generate_content(
        model="gemini-3.7-flash",
        contents=f"{SCHEMA_PROMPT}\n\nQuery: {query}",
        config={
            "response_mime_type": "application/json",
            "response_schema": Judgement,
            "temperature": 0,  # same query, same verdict, week after week
        },
    )
    return Judgement.model_validate_json(response.text)


AMBIGUOUS = [
    "rork.com core business features target audience",
    "onspace vs rork for complex app logic",
]

for q in AMBIGUOUS:
    j = adjudicate(q)
    # Low-confidence calls are not adopted; they carry over to next week
    verdict = "human" if j.likely_human else "fanout"
    status = verdict if j.confidence >= 0.7 else "hold"
    print(f"{status:7s} conf={j.confidence:.2f}  {q}\n        {j.reason}")
```

This assumes Python 3.10+, `google-genai` and `pydantic`. Passing a Pydantic class as `response_schema` follows Google's Structured outputs documentation as of November 2026; `response_json_schema` with a raw JSON Schema also works. I pin `temperature` to 0 because if the same phrase gets a different verdict each week, the week-over-week comparison stops meaning anything.

Asking for `confidence` and refusing anything under 0.7 matters more than it looks. Models return a confident sentence whether or not they are confident. If you do not force the hesitation into a number, the hesitation never reaches your records.

Carrying rows over as unresolved is the correct outcome, not a failure. Forcing a verdict on something you cannot judge leaves the reasoning nowhere while the aggregation marches on. The same instinct shows up in [normalising a closed label vocabulary](/articles/gemini-advanced/gemini-closed-vocabulary-near-miss-normalization-layer-field-notes), and the schema side borrows directly from [structured output validation](/articles/gemini-api/gemini-api-structured-output-schema-validation-fix).

## Three exclusion policies, three different CTRs

This was the part that changed how I report the number at all.

Recomputing effective CTR under different exclusion ranges, starting from 0.70% over 41,700 impressions:

From 0.70% to 0.81%, on policy alone.

If I have to pick one, I report "score 3 and above only." It is decided by the deterministic rule alone, so anyone who reruns it gets the same number. The figure that also drops ambiguous rows inherits whatever wobble the model has that week, so I keep it for internal discussion only.

So I stopped reporting "effective CTR" as a single number. Give someone a bare figure and they will ask which one is real, and the answer lives entirely in the exclusion policy.

Now I write the two together in one sentence: "0.73%, excluding score-3-and-above only." Split them across two lines and by next week the number is travelling alone.

## Do not delete the human query that got zero clicks

Row four, `expo audio continue playing screen locked react native 2025`, landed on the human side of the scorer. It is nine tokens long, but every one of them describes a real problem: keeping audio playing while the screen is locked. Anyone who has shipped background audio will recognise the word order.

1,023 impressions. Zero clicks.

That is not denominator pollution. That is **a real failure to be chosen** — either the position is far enough down that nobody scrolls to it, or the title does not meet the query where it is. Either way it needs its own investigation.

This is the risk in cleaning a denominator. While you sweep, things that were never meant to be swept disappear from view. Excluding synthetic queries exists to make CTR readable, not to make zero-click rows stop bothering you.

I split the script's output in two, so excluded queries always land in their own section. Set aside, not deleted. That one change is what lets me pick this row back up next week.

## One script, one line per policy

The numbers in that table were not worked out by hand. They come from running the verdicts through the script below, and the problem it solves is a mundane one: the number and the policy ending up in different places. By forcing the policy name into the same output line as the CTR, it becomes awkward to quote the number without its context.

```python
# effective_ctr.py - turn verdicts into one-line effective CTR per exclusion policy
import sys

TOTAL_IMPRESSIONS = 41_700
TOTAL_CLICKS = 292

# (query, impressions, clicks, band) - copied from fanout_score.py / adjudicate.py
ROWS = [
    ("rork.com core business features target audience", 1371, 0, "ambiguous"),
    ("rork max swiftui features and ai capabilities", 1282, 0, "fanout"),
    ("onspace vs rork for complex app logic", 1149, 0, "ambiguous"),
    ("expo audio continue playing screen locked react native 2025", 1023, 0, "human"),
    ("rork max swiftui features and native app generation", 624, 0, "fanout"),
]

POLICIES = {
    "none": set(),
    "fanout-only": {"fanout"},
    "fanout+ambiguous": {"fanout", "ambiguous"},
}


def report(policy: str) -> str:
    bands = POLICIES[policy]
    removed = [r for r in ROWS if r[3] in bands]
    removed_imp = sum(r[1] for r in removed)
    removed_clicks = sum(r[2] for r in removed)
    imp = TOTAL_IMPRESSIONS - removed_imp
    ctr = (TOTAL_CLICKS - removed_clicks) / imp * 100
    # Number and policy always share one line, so the number can't travel alone
    return (f"policy={policy}: effective CTR {ctr:.2f}% "
            f"(removed {removed_imp:,} impressions / {len(removed)} queries, "
            f"{removed_imp / TOTAL_IMPRESSIONS:.1%} of total)")


if __name__ == "__main__":
    for p in (sys.argv[1:] or POLICIES):
        print(report(p))
    # Excluded rows are set aside in their own section, never deleted
    print("--- set aside (not deleted) ---")
    for q, i, c, b in ROWS:
        if b != "human":
            print(f"{b:9s} {i:>6,}  {q}")
    print("--- human, zero clicks (next week's candidates) ---")
    for q, i, c, b in ROWS:
        if b == "human" and c == 0:
            print(f"{i:>6,}  {q}")
```

Here is the output on Python 3.10, run with no arguments:

```text

policy=fanout-only: effective CTR 0.73% (removed 1,906 impressions / 2 queries, 4.6% of total)
policy=fanout+ambiguous: effective CTR 0.78% (removed 4,426 impressions / 4 queries, 10.6% of total)
--- set aside (not deleted) ---
ambiguous  1,371  rork.com core business features target audience
fanout     1,282  rork max swiftui features and ai capabilities
ambiguous  1,149  onspace vs rork for complex app logic
fanout       624  rork max swiftui features and native app generation
--- human, zero clicks (next week's candidates) ---
 1,023  expo audio continue playing screen locked react native 2025
```

`TOTAL_CLICKS` is the period's total click count. Working backwards from 0.70% over 41,700 impressions also gives 292, so I confirmed the two agree before trusting the table. The script subtracts the excluded rows' clicks from the numerator as well as their impressions from the denominator — if a row with clicks is ever classified as synthetic, CTR should not come out higher than reality.

## A triage table for high-impression, zero-click queries

When zero-click rows crowd the top of the list, denominator pollution is only one possible cause. These are the cases I have actually run into, in the order I check them.

<table>
<thead>
<tr><th>Symptom</th><th>Likely cause</th><th>How to check</th><th>What to do</th></tr>
</thead>
<tbody>
<tr><td>Long query of stacked attribute nouns, high impressions, zero clicks</td><td>Sub-query generated by AI search fan-out</td><td>Does fanout_score.py put it in fanout or ambiguous?</td><td>Exclude from the denominator, report with the policy name, keep the row in a set-aside section</td></tr>
<tr><td>Query that spells out a real problem, high impressions, zero clicks</td><td>Ranking too low to be seen</td><td>Filter to that query alone and read its average position</td><td>Rewrite the matching article's heading and opening around that exact problem</td></tr>
<tr><td>Good average position, still zero clicks</td><td>Counted as one link inside an AI answer block</td><td>Search it yourself and see whether you appear as a normal result or inside the block</td><td>Instead of retitling, put a quotable passage (definition plus steps) in the body</td></tr>
<tr><td>Same query gets a different verdict each week</td><td>Model variance, temperature not pinned</td><td>Run adjudicate.py three times on the same query and compare</td><td>Set temperature to 0 and carry anything under 0.7 confidence over as unresolved</td></tr>
<tr><td>Effective CTR jumps around week to week</td><td>The exclusion policy itself keeps changing</td><td>Does each record name its policy?</td><td>Fix one reporting policy and keep policy and number in one line</td></tr>
</tbody>
</table>

Rows two and three are real problems that no amount of denominator cleaning will fix. Working down the table top to bottom separates the rows to set aside from the rows you actually need to face.

## Folding it into the weekly routine

Five fixed steps:

1. Export Search Console queries sorted by impressions, descending
2. Run `fanout_score.py` and split into `fanout` / `ambiguous` / `human`
3. Run `adjudicate.py` **only** in weeks where `ambiguous` exceeds ten rows
4. Record effective CTR with `effective_ctr.py`, as a single line that names the exclusion policy
5. Push zero-click rows from the `human` band into a separate list for next week

Step three is conditional on purpose. Make the model call permanent and the judgement criteria quietly migrate into the model. Weeks where the scorer alone was enough are evidence that the criteria are holding.

The reporting side dropped straight onto the [Streamlit and Gemini API dashboard](/articles/gemini-api/gemini-api-streamlit-personal-blog-ops-dashboard-gsc-ga4) I built earlier — excluded impressions plotted underneath the total impressions chart.

## Where it breaks next: verdicts going stale

Run this for a few weeks and the next problem arrives: last quarter's verdicts are not guaranteed to hold this quarter. The way AI search fans out keeps changing, and human queries are getting longer too, nudged by the habit of talking to assistants in full sentences. The more natural-language searches people type, the more a token-count scorer like mine will push genuine human queries toward the synthetic side.

Once a month, I pull every `human` row that has at least one click and check whether any `fanout` verdicts have crept in among similar phrasing — and, the other way round, whether any row with clicks has been labelled `fanout`. If it has, the rule has aged. Clicks never feed the classifier, but they are allowed to **audit** it. Keeping that order intact is the line I hold so the metric cannot quietly bend toward what I want.

The idea of keeping labels stable across weeks is something I also wrote about in [validating and retrying on schema drift](/articles/gemini-api/gemini-api-structured-output-schema-drift-validation-retry-field-notes); it is worth a look once verdict wobble starts to bother you.

## Closing

Sort your own impressions descending and read the top ten rows with your eyes. That alone tells you what is sitting in your denominator.

Until I did this, I had been reading a falling CTR as a headline problem. What had actually changed was the thing being measured. As AI search keeps expanding, I suspect this kind of drift accumulates quietly.

When a metric refuses to move, the denominator may deserve suspicion before the strategy does.
