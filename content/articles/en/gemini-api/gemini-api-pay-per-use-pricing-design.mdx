The question I hear most often from indie developers building a Gemini API SaaS is "how much should I charge?" Price it too low and you bleed cash on heavy users. Price it too high and no one subscribes. Most people resolve this by copying a competitor: "let's just do 10 USD per month." That decision is usually where margin death spirals begin.

This article walks through a pricing method that starts from cost, not from competitors. I have designed pricing for several AI-powered services, and whether you do this calculation up front determines, more than almost anything else, whether you are profitable six months later.

## Why "just 10 USD per month" breaks

Let's run actual numbers. As of April 2026, Gemini 2.5 Flash pay-as-you-go pricing is 0.10 USD per million input tokens and 0.40 USD per million output tokens.

Suppose one user generates "5,000 input tokens × 200 requests" and "1,000 output tokens × 200 requests" per month. Monthly cost per user works out to:

- Input: 5,000 × 200 = 1M tokens → 0.10 USD
- Output: 1,000 × 200 = 200k tokens → 0.08 USD
- Total: about 0.18 USD per user per month

Looking at this alone, "10 USD per month is 98% margin, easy win" seems obvious. The reality diverges fast. Once you actually launch, usage distribution is never uniform.

## The "10% of users eat 80% of cost" phenomenon

Across nearly every indie-developer SaaS I have watched, usage follows a Pareto-like distribution. The top 10% of users consume 70-80% of the tokens.

Say 100 paying users average 0.18 USD cost each. In practice, 10 of them might cost 1.5 USD each, while the other 90 cost 0.04 USD each.

Total cost: 10 × 1.5 + 90 × 0.04 = 18.6 USD
Average cost: 18.6 / 100 = 0.186 USD per user

The average is "as expected," but the trap is there. At 10 USD per month, heavy users net 8.5 USD. Fine. But the moment a user even heavier than your current top 10% appears — what people call an "outlier" — they can single-handedly generate negative margin of several dollars per month.

The biggest risk of a flat-rate SaaS is these outliers. If you do not design for them in pricing, every heavier user you attract makes you less profitable.

## The profitability equation — four variables

Price design needs four variables.

1. **CPU** (Cost Per User) — the average monthly cost per user
2. **CPU_HEAVY** — the cost of users in the top 10% (typically 8-10x CPU)
3. **PRICE** — the monthly price you set
4. **MARGIN_TARGET** — your target gross margin (I recommend at least 70% for indie SaaS)

The relationship you want to hold is:

```
PRICE >= CPU_HEAVY × (1 / (1 - MARGIN_TARGET))
```

In plain English: set the minimum price so that even heavy users stay within your margin target. With CPU_HEAVY = 1.5 USD and a 70% margin target, the floor is 1.5 / 0.3 = 5 USD per month.

"Average cost is only 0.18 USD, so 10 USD is plenty" turns out to be dangerously optimistic.

## Capped flat-rate or true pay-per-use

Once you do this calculation, you often realize a flat-rate SaaS simply does not work. The two realistic options from there:

**Capped flat-rate.** Charge 10 USD per month, but cap monthly usage by request count or total tokens. For example: "200 requests per month, 0.10 USD per request beyond that." This prevents heavy users from eroding margin.

**True pay-per-use.** Users prepay credits and burn them as they use the service. Stripe offers a customer-balance primitive, or you can manage credits in your own database. This fits unpredictable usage patterns better — which is why it is the default model for developer and enterprise customers.

In my experience: capped flat-rate fits consumer-facing SaaS; true pay-per-use fits developer or enterprise SaaS.

## Pricing anchors — align with psychological tiers

Once you have a minimum price from cost analysis, adjust upward to a tier users psychologically accept. The tiers I observe for consumer-facing AI SaaS in 2026 are roughly:

- Easy-to-try: 5-10 USD per month
- Serious: 15-30 USD per month
- Premium: 50-100 USD per month

Under 10 USD feels like a try, but margin pressure grows. Above 30 USD feels like a committed decision, which filters your market down but selects more engaged users.

If your cost-based floor came out to 5 USD, I would usually set price at 9.99 USD — "easy to try" psychologically, while comfortably maintaining 70% gross margin.

## Pricing for churn reality

Churn is the other half of pricing, and it often gets ignored.

Consumer-facing SaaS averages 5-10% monthly churn. That means out of 100 paying users, 5-10 leave each month. If your cost to acquire one user (ads, content, support) is 5 USD, and churn is 10%, you must acquire 100 new users per month just to replace those lost — that's 500 USD of acquisition cost per month.

LTV = price × average retention months. At 10 USD/month and 10% churn, average retention is 1 / 0.10 = 10 months, so LTV = 100 USD. Subtract acquisition cost of 5 USD, and gross LTV is 95 USD. Subtract API cost (roughly 1.8 USD over 10 months), and net LTV is about 93 USD per user.

Raise the price to 15 USD/month and churn may tick up to 12%, but LTV rises to 15 × 8.3 = 124 USD. At the same acquisition cost, raising the price increases total profit.

## Automating cost analysis with Claude Code

You do not need to run these calculations manually. Ask Claude Code to write Supabase queries that surface cost distribution in real time:

```
From the usage_logs table, compute the following for the last 30 days:
1. Total tokens consumed per user
2. Average cost for the top 10% of users
3. Median-user cost
4. Overall average cost

Output each as USD and JPY (assume 150 JPY / USD).
Also compute the minimum monthly price required to maintain a 70% gross margin.
```

Schedule that query to run daily and post results to Slack. Gemini API pricing will shift over time, so keeping a cost-based monitor running gives you early warning if you ever drift out of profitability.

## Pricing is calculated, not decided

Most indie SaaS products that fail to turn a profit set their price by intuition or competitor comparison. Once you get used to backing price out of cost, you can decide before launch whether the product is viable at the intended price — saving months of building on a bad foundation.
