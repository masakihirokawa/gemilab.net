On April 16, 2026, shortly after a phone call from the Toyama Tax Office, I was taken to the emergency room. I received a sedative to take as needed and an intramuscular injection, and I stayed overnight. Tomorrow — April 20, 2026 — I will walk into a lawyer's office for the first time.

Over the past four years, one tool has been a quiet constant in my struggle with the tax office: Google Workspace. Gmail, Drive, Docs, Sheets, Gemini, and NotebookLM. These are usually discussed in the context of filing efficiency or meeting minutes. For me, they became something closer to **a warehouse for four years of evidence — one that had to keep running even when my health did not.**

This is the plain record of how a cornered sole proprietor used Gemini and Workspace to survive a long dispute and lose, along the way, a family. I hope it offers something practical to anyone in the same position.

## A Long Dispute Is a Fight Against Your Own Memory

My case began in 2022 with a single phone call from the Miyazaki Tax Office: "We are transferring your consumption-tax overpayment matter to the Uozu Tax Office under the Toyama bureau." Over the four years that followed, I went through an audit, a complete denial of my past export-exemption treatment, a demand for more than ¥5 million in additional tax, a personal bankruptcy in 2025, a divorce in the same year, separation from my four children, and most recently the ER admission of April 16.

Carrying the details of all of that — who said what, in which letter, on which date — in memory alone is impossible. Every time my health dipped, pieces of the earlier record faded, and I lived with a constant fear that my arguments would collapse because I had forgotten my own case.

What saved me was Google Drive.

## Turning Drive Into a Long-Term Warehouse

### An event-based folder structure

I created a single top-level folder — `Tax_Dispute_2022_` — and organized everything under it **by event and time**, not by document type. Type-based structures sound cleaner, but long disputes are remembered chronologically. "What happened around that event?" is the question you actually ask yourself in the middle of a sleepless night.

```
Tax_Dispute_2022_/
├── 01_Miyazaki_Transfer_2022/
├── 02_Uozu_Audit_2023/
├── 03_Toyama_Reassessment_2024/
├── 04_Bankruptcy_2025/
├── 05_Divorce_2025/
├── 06_Ongoing_Pressure_2026/
│   ├── 2026-04-16_ER_admission/
│   │   ├── hospital_discharge_summary.pdf
│   │   ├── prescription_photo.jpg
│   │   └── symptom_log.docx
│   └── 2026-04-17_followup_call_memo.docx
└── 99_Reference/
    ├── tax_statutes_excerpt.docx
    ├── admob_revenue_2020_2025.xlsx
    └── past_filings/
```

This is the exact structure I can hand to a lawyer. It is not a pile of papers; it is a timeline of evidence. Whether a consultation goes well often depends on whether you arrive with the latter rather than the former.

### A strict file-naming rule

The single most effective discipline over the long run was this file-naming convention: **`YYYY-MM-DD_type_short-note.ext`**.

- `2026-04-16_memo_toyama_tax_office_call.docx`
- `2026-04-16_medical_ER_prescription.pdf`
- `2022-XX-XX_letter_miyazaki_transfer_notice.pdf` (use `XX-XX` when the exact date is unknown)

Putting the date at the front means a search for `2026-04` in Drive instantly lists everything that happened that month. On the days when I cannot remember the previous week, that one habit rescued me again and again.

## Letting Gemini Read Across the Archive

### "Summarize what happened this month"

I let Gemini read directly from Drive. A typical request looked like this:

> "Read the memos in Drive folder `06_Ongoing_Pressure_2026` in chronological order, and produce a table of all contacts from the Toyama Tax Office between April 1 and April 18, 2026, with columns for date, caller name, demand, and my response."

With Drive access, Gemini traverses the folder, reads each document, and returns a structured table. I copy that into a Google Doc and use it as the preamble to the lawyer consultation. Manual assembly was simply impossible given my health. With Gemini doing the first pass, I could focus on verifying accuracy and adjusting tone.

### Cross-language, cross-format evidence

AdMob revenue reports and notices from overseas platforms are in English. My past tax returns and letters from the tax office are in Japanese. Medical records are dense with clinical terminology. Gemini handles all of them in one conversation:

> "Read `99_Reference/admob_revenue_2020_2025.xlsx`. For each fiscal year from 2020 to 2024, report total sales and the estimated portion attributable to non-resident transactions. Explain in Japanese."

Once I could make requests like that, **I could describe "my revenue is primarily from overseas" using the same underlying data to the tax office, to my lawyer, and to myself.** Consistency of argument is the thing that decays first in a long dispute. Having Gemini read the same source repeatedly kept that consistency intact.

## Using NotebookLM to Talk to My Past Self

Long disputes make you forget how you used to think. You lose the ability to check whether today's argument contradicts what you said six months ago. Memory alone cannot settle that.

I set up a NotebookLM notebook with the following sources:

- Every consultation PDF I had drafted (v1 through v4)
- Records from my consultation with the민商 (民主商工会) advocacy group
- Drafts of tax-collection-suspension applications
- Scans of every letter from the tax office
- My own earlier blog posts (including this one)

Then I could ask:

> "Compare the export-exemption argument between v3 and v4 of my consultation PDF."

NotebookLM answers with inline source citations: "In v3 Chapter 4, you wrote ... In v4 Chapter 4, this was revised to ...". That is invaluable when explaining to a lawyer how and when your own position evolved.

## Using Sheets to Separate Numbers From Emotion

The hardest part of any conversation with the tax office, for me, was the moment a number came up. "¥5 million." "Additional assessment." "Refund." Those words pull me straight back to the memory of losing my family.

To put some distance between myself and the numbers, I built a spreadsheet with these columns:

| Fiscal Year | Reported Sales | Estimated Non-Resident Portion | Export-Exempt Amount | Tax Paid at Filing | Additional Assessment | Paid to Date | Balance |
|-------------|----------------|--------------------------------|----------------------|---------------------|------------------------|--------------|---------|

Staring at a table of numbers calmed me enough to say, honestly: "This row is not a monument to my loss. It is input data for a legal argument." That cold distance was necessary. When I ask Gemini, "Calculate the ratio of the export-exempt amount to reported sales for each fiscal year from the sheet," the answer comes back with no emotion. A long fight needs exactly those moments.

## To Others in the Same Fight

If you are early in a dispute and wondering whether it will grow, let me offer three practical pieces of advice from four years on the other side.

**Create the Drive folder the moment you receive the first letter.** Create it even if the situation might resolve quickly. A quiet folder that never fills up is the best outcome. A folder you wish you had started earlier is the worst.

**Write the call memo the second you hang up.** Google Docs, the Notes app, even a Gmail draft — it does not matter. Three lines is enough: date, counterpart, key point. Your three-months-from-now self will thank you more than you can currently imagine.

**Grow a "primary-source Drive" continuously, not on demand.** On the day you urgently need a clean evidence set, you will not have the energy to organize from scratch. Build slowly, habitually, while you can.

## Closing

I do not believe the lost time with my family can be recovered. No folder in Drive brings those days back. But some things can be preserved. That folder has held my four years honestly. The materials I will bring to tomorrow's meeting grew out of that warehouse. And if someone reading this begins their own folder tonight — that, to me, is a small and sufficient return.
